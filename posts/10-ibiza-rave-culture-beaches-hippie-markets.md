# Ibiza, Both Islands: The Morning Raves, the Planes, and the Calas Worth the Climb

[IMAGE]

Ibiza is not one island. It is at least two, sharing a coastline and refusing to acknowledge each other.

In the one, a pool party starts at one in the afternoon with a sound system loud enough to be felt in your sternum, and the person next to you is drinking a €20 cocktail in a rented bed. In the other, a dirt track above the water ends in a pine-shadowed cove where the only sound is a scooter going the other way, and you swim because the water is cold and clear, and that is the entire event.

Both are real. Both are the same island. And the fastest way to ruin a trip here is to pick one and treat the other as a day off.

## The island's actual origin story

Before the superclubs there were hippies — arrivistes in the 1960s and 70s escaping various national moods, settling inland in the pine hills around San Carlos and San Juan where land was cheap and nobody asked what you were doing.

Nightlife grew alongside them, not the other way round. **Amnesia**, **Pacha**, **Ku** (today **Privilege**) and **Es Paradis** opened in the 70s and 80s as places where the music was whatever the DJ felt like playing — jazz, disco, rock, Italian pop, all in one set. That refusal to specialise is what people later named the **Balearic beat**, and **DJ Alfredo**'s sets at Amnesia are the reference point for it. The 1987 British press pilgrimage to the island is the reason electronic dance music looks the way it does now.

What this means practically: when you stand in a queue at a club here, you're standing in the tail end of something that started as an accident of cheap land and strange neighbours. It reads better than it sells.

## The morning rave: O Beach and the day club

[IMAGE]

San Antonio's **O Beach** is the most honest expression of this island. It opened in 2012 as a proper **day club** — pool in the middle, sunbeds around it, party running while the sun is still high — and it has stayed at it for over a decade, which in Ibiza is a geological age.

You arrive in swimwear with something to throw on over it. You book a bed or you don't. The music is commercial house, aimed at people who want to be able to sing along, and the entertainment is loud and unsubtle. It is not cool and it is not trying to be. It is a very specific pleasure: daytime, water, volume, no pretence of being night.

**Best for:** the afternoon start, the pool-in-swimwear economics, a first nightclub experience that doesn't require staying up until 5am.

**The honest caveat:** this is San Antonio, not Dalt Vila. If your image of Ibiza is stone walls and faded glamour, you've arrived in the wrong half of the island and about three kilometres from the sunset strip. Which is its own thing — see below.

## The planes: Ushuaïa and Playa d'en Bossa

Ibiza's airport sits at the edge of **Playa d'en Bossa**, and the approach path runs low over the beach. Somebody eventually noticed that a widebody passing a few hundred feet overhead is a genuinely thrilling sound, and built a club directly underneath it.

That club is **Ushuaïa** — open-air, a stage facing a central pool ringed with palm trees, planes skimming over the middle of the set. It is the only place I know where the aircraft schedule is part of the production. It opens while it's still light and runs late; it is not a traditional dark-room club and doesn't behave like one.

[IMAGE]

Directly alongside it is **Hï Ibiza**, the night-side counterpart, built on the site of the old **Space** — which for a long time was the island's most famous after-hours room. So Playa d'en Bossa gives you a clean pair: daylight and open air at Ushuaïa, then the proper dark hours next door without touching a taxi. **DC-10**, a few minutes down the same road, is where the very long nights end if you have that in you.

**The practical bit that saves you money and dignity:** pre-book tickets, arrive at opening rather than peak, and take the bus. Driving to Playa d'en Bossa after midnight means circling car parks in the dark while the person who booked a bed walks in.

## The dolce vita, and where it actually lives

The glamour half isn't the club strip. It's **Ibiza Town** — specifically **Dalt Vila**, the walled upper town that is a UNESCO World Heritage site, and the **marina** below it, where superyachts sit stern-to and the restaurant tables are set within reach of the gangways.

This is where the island's *dolce vita* is actually spent: long lunches on the water, a slow walk uphill through the portals of the walls, the cathedral square at the top with the whole harbour laid out underneath, and dinner at ten. It is the oldest part of the island and the least interested in your itinerary.

[IMAGE]

San Antonio's **sunset strip** — the café bars facing the water, the names everyone knows — is the other end of the same impulse. It's crowded, it's photographed to death, and it still works: the sun sets behind the islands out there and a few hundred people applaud. Go once, early enough to get a seat, then eat somewhere quiet.

## The west coast: the calas worth the climb

West and north-west of San Antonio is where the island stops performing. Pine-forested hills, small coves, few services.

**Cala Gració** is the easy one — a shallow, sheltered bay just north of San Antonio, smooth water, no drama, the right choice with children or on a windy day.

[IMAGE]

**Cala Salada and Cala Saladeta** are the ones people mean when they say the west is the best of the island. Two coves cut into limestone under pine, the water clear to the rock, and **Saladeta** — the smaller of the two — wilder, service-free, reached by a short scramble over the rocks at the far end of Cala Salada. No beds, no bar, nothing to buy. Bring water.

Both are protected, and in high season car access is restricted for that reason. The system that works:

- **Minibus P7** from San Antonio bus station, roughly every 30 minutes between about 09:30 and 20:30 in summer
- **Park and ride** at the **Can Coix sports centre**, where there's a large car park
- **Ferry** from San Antonio port as a third option

However you arrive, the last stretch to the water is on foot — about five minutes down from the bus stop. The car park at the cove itself is tiny and closes when it's full. Be there before ten, or accept that the walk is the price of the beach being worth it.

## Atlantis: the place you have probably seen and should not sneak into

[IMAGE]

**Atlantis** is not its real name. The place is **Sa Pedrera de Cala d'Hort** — a former sandstone quarry on the cliffs near **Cala d'Hort**, in the south-west, under the shadow of **Es Vedrà**. The squared blocks cut out of it gave Ibiza Town some of its stone; the sea later filled the cuts, and the result is a set of rectangular tidal pools and carved chambers that photographs extremely well and has therefore been ruined, in the usual sequence, by being photographed extremely well.

Here is the part most write-ups leave out. Access has been progressively closed. The **Es Vedrà viewpoint is now permanently closed**, residents around Cala d'Hort have physically blocked routes to the area, and both the quarry and the surrounding protected land are subject to enforcement — including fines for trespass, and a police investigation into an unlicensed party held there. This is not a case of a "locals' secret"; it's a case of a place that has been loved to death and is now legally out of bounds.

**So:** if you want to see it, go with a licensed guide who has the paperwork, or accept the view from the legal side. Do not scramble down a closed path, and do not mistake a legal grey area for permission. The most beautiful thing about the south-west is that it is still there at all.

## The hippie markets — originals only

The markets are the hippie legacy in commercial form, and two of them are genuinely worth the drive.

**Punta Arabí, Es Canar (east coast)** — running on **Wednesdays since 1973**, and with over 400 stalls, the biggest on the island. This is the original, and it feels like it: a huge sprawl of leather, silver, textiles, food and tat, with live music in the middle. It is loud and it is a market, in the total sense.

**Las Dalias, San Carlos** — on the **Sant Carles road at km 12**, holding its main market **Saturdays, roughly 10:00 to 20:00, free entry**, with night-market evenings on other days. Smaller, prettier, and closer to the spirit: the island's inland hippie heartland around San Carlos is where you can see what all of this grew out of.

[IMAGE]

And the third, which is not a market so much as a weekly ritual: **Cala Benirràs**, on the north coast, where drummers gather at the water's edge on **Sunday evenings** and play into the sunset. It is the most Ibiza thing on the island and it costs nothing, which is why it survives.

**Best for:** Wednesdays and Saturdays, if you're planning the week around it. Both markets are a half-day each, and neither is near the beach you were going to anyway.

## The practical bit

- **Car:** you need one for the west calas, the markets and the south-west. You do not need one for Playa d'en Bossa or Ibiza Town — buses run and parking is a punishment.
- **Season:** June and September are the sweet spot. August gives you the full programme and the full crowds; the calas are unbearable by eleven.
- **Tickets:** for O Beach, Ushuaïa and Hï, book ahead. Walk-up prices at the door in August are a tax on optimism.
- **Water:** the wild coves have no services. Take more than you think.
- **The two-island problem:** don't schedule a 6am club night before a 9am beach. The island punishes that specific plan every single summer.

## What I'd do differently

I'd stop trying to do both islands in one day, which is the only mistake that actually ruins this place. Nights in Playa d'en Bossa, days in the west, and Ibiza Town in between as the hinge — lunch in the marina, the walk up to Dalt Vila, then out. And I'd go to Benirràs on the Sunday and skip whatever club I was going to skip anyway.
